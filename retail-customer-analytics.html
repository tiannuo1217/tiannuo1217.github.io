Who to keep, who to email: a retail customer analytics case study

Two public retail datasets, one question a CRM team asks every week: which customers are worth the next pound or dollar of marketing, and does the email we send actually change behavior?

01Data and cleaning

UCI Online Retail II holds every line item sold by a UK online giftware retailer from 1 Dec 2009 to 9 Dec 2011; many of its customers are small wholesalers. Before any analysis, I loaded the raw file into SQLite and applied five cleaning rules in order, counting what each one removed.

The biggest cut is rows with no customer ID (23% of the raw file). They can't be tied to a person, so they can't feed retention or segmentation. Cancellations matter too: of identified invoices were cancellations, so revenue would be overstated without the returns filter. The clean table has line items, customers and orders.

02Do new customers come back?

I grouped customers by the month of their first order and measured what share ordered again in each following month.

For the January–November 2010 cohorts, ordered again in month 1, in month 6 and in month 12. Most of the drop-off happens before the second order; after that, retention is flat.

* A data trap worth catching. The December 2009 cohort looks far more loyal ( in month 1). It isn't a better cohort: the data starts that month, so its "new" customers include long-standing ones who simply hadn't been seen yet (left-censoring). I left it out of the averages above.

04Which cohorts are worth more?

Retention says whether customers return; lifetime value says what they're worth. I tracked cumulative revenue per acquired customer for the 2010 quarterly cohorts over their first 12 months.

All four cohorts spent a similar amount in their first month (£440–£490). By month 12 the Q4 cohort (October–December, the holiday season) had reached , against for Q2 and Q3. That is 22–27% less. Q1 2010 is higher still, but it sits right after the data start, so some of its customers are probably returning customers rather than new ones; I don't lean on it.

05Segmenting customers, and checking it works

I scored every customer on recency and frequency as of 9 June 2011, using bands a marketing team can act on (ordered in the last 30 / 90 / 180 / 365 days; 1, 2, 3–4, 5–9 or 10+ orders), and grouped them into six segments. Then I checked the segments against what customers actually did over the next six months, data the segmentation never saw.

Champions (ordered in the last 90 days, 5+ orders) are 23% of customers but produced 71% of next-6-month revenue, and 90% of them ordered again. The group to act on is At risk: 13% of customers who used to buy often (median 4 orders, historical spend close to Loyal customers) but hadn't ordered in over six months. Fewer than half came back. That's the win-back list.

06Can a model beat the rules?

Segments are easy to act on but coarse: everyone in a segment gets the same score. I built a model to rank customers one by one, and tested it the way it would actually be used. It was trained on what was known on 9 Dec 2010 (and who ordered over the next six months), then scored customers as of 9 Jun 2011 and was checked against what they did through December. The test period is later and includes the holidays, so this is an out-of-time test, not a random split.

The features are all behavioral and built in SQL: recency, frequency, total spend, average order value, spend in the last 90 days, tenure, average days between orders (a LAG window), product breadth, cancellation rate, share of orders in Oct–Dec, and UK vs. overseas. I compared three scorers: the RFM segments from section 05, a logistic regression and gradient boosting.

The logistic regression beat the RFM rules on every measure (AUC vs. ; the gain is , 95% bootstrap interval ). Gradient boosting did no better than the simpler model, so I'd ship the logistic regression: it is easier to explain, and easier to monitor.

The ranking held up; the probabilities didn't. The model under-predicted every decile, by about at the bottom. More customers came back in the test window, which includes the holidays ( vs. in training). This is the drift a deployed model has to be monitored for. The fix is to recalibrate before each season, or to train on the same season a year earlier.

Total spend and number of orders carry most of the signal. Two less obvious drivers: holding total spend fixed, a higher average order value and a longer tenure both lower the odds of a repeat order. That fits large one-off orders from customers who never become regulars. And the model sharpens the win-back list from section 05: within the At-risk customers it barely predicts who will return ( vs. ), but the top half by score holds of the segment's next-6-month revenue. Start the win-back list there.

07Does the email work? An A/B test readout

The MineThatData experiment randomly split 64,000 recent customers into three groups: a men's merchandise email, a women's merchandise email, or no email. Visits, conversions and spend were tracked for two weeks.

Before reading results I checked the test was sound. Group sizes matched the planned one-third split (sample-ratio test p = ), and every pre-test variable was balanced across groups (largest standardized difference , well under the usual 0.1 threshold).

Both emails worked, and the men's email won on every metric: conversion rose from 0.57% to 1.25% and spend per customer by $0.77. Spend per buyer barely changed (), so the lift comes from more people buying, not bigger baskets.

08Who responds?

I split customers by what they had bought in the past year and whether they were new.

The men's email matched or beat the women's email in five of six segments, even among customers who had only bought women's merchandise. The women's email was only marginally ahead for new women's-only buyers. Existing customers who had only bought men's products barely responded to either email; with a real cost per send, they are the first group to suppress.

09Does targeting beat a blanket send?

To avoid grading my own homework, I split customers randomly in half. On the first half I picked the best email for each segment; on the second half I estimated what each policy would have earned (inverse-propensity weighting, which works because assignment was random).

The targeted rule (women's email for new women's-only buyers, men's email for everyone else) earned about 20% more than sending the men's email to everyone. But the difference is per customer with a 95% interval of , so it could still be zero. Spend is noisy because 99% of customers spend nothing; confirming a gain this size would take about customers per arm. My recommendation is to ship the men's email as the default and run one focused test of the women's email on new women's-only buyers, using conversion as the primary metric.

Recommendations

  1. Make the second order the KPI, and time it. Run the post-purchase sequence between day 30 and day 90, and report 90-day repeat rate by acquisition channel.
  2. Protect Champions, win back At-risk, ranked by the model. Champions carry 71% of future revenue. Among the 665 At-risk customers, start with the top half by model score, which holds most of the segment's future revenue.
  3. Monitor the model, not just the campaign. Check calibration before each holiday season and recalibrate when the base rate moves.
  4. Budget Q4 acquisition on a lower LTV. Holiday cohorts are worth 22–27% less over a year; CAC targets should reflect that.
  5. Email: men's creative by default, one focused follow-up test. Suppress existing men's-only buyers once send costs are in the model.

Limitations

  • Both datasets are public and old (2008–2011), and they come from different businesses; the methods carry over, the exact numbers don't.
  • No cost or margin data, so the email analysis is about revenue, not profit.
  • RFM bands were chosen to be easy to act on, not optimized. The repurchase model uses behavioral features only (no marketing touches or product categories); a probabilistic CLV model (BG/NBD with Gamma-Gamma) would add a spend forecast.
  • The experiment measured two weeks of behavior; longer-term effects such as unsubscribes aren't observed.
